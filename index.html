Abstract

Egocentric vision offers a first-person view of human perception and decision making, yet its potential for traffic-safety prediction remains underexplored. In this work, we study the decoding of pedestrian crossing intentions from short egocentric video clips. We approach this by formulating the task as a closed-ended visual question answering (VQA) problem and leveraging vision language models (VLMs) to predict the pedestrians' intent. We first benchmark three families of state-of-the-art VLMs in a zero-shot setting, finding that they achieve moderate gains over random guessing but exhibit limited higher-level traffic reasoning. Motivated by these findings, we further adapt VLMs to the target task using parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Our results show that the fine-tuned models substantially outperform their zero-shot counterparts and achieve a 9% accuracy improvement over a specialized transformer-based baseline. Finally, we demonstrate that incorporating additional contextual cues — ego motion, vehicle motion, and eye gaze — further improves predictive performance. In particular, the fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B model guided by eye gaze and ego motion achieves a 14.5% accuracy improvement over the transformer baseline, establishing a new state of the art for egocentric pedestrian intent decoding.

Approach

We frame crossing-intention prediction as a closed-ended VQA task: given a 2-second egocentric video clip, the model answers "Will you cross or yield in the next second?" This lets us plug in any VLM off the shelf — and fine-tune it with minimal overhead.

🎥 Input

8 uniformly sampled frames from a 2-second clip, interleaved with timestamps. Optionally augmented with eye-gaze fixations rendered as red dots, plus text context: ego speed, vehicle position, and personal attributes.

🤖 Models

Two families: MLLMs (Qwen3-VL-2B/8B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, InternVL3-2B/8B) with broad web-scale knowledge, and a VLP (GroundVQA) explicitly pretrained on egocentric video-text pairs.

🔍 Adaptation

Zero-shot evaluation with standard, chain-of-thought, and visual (set-of-marks) prompting. Then LoRA fine-tuning on domain-specific QA pairs — freezing the vision encoder, updating only language and cross-modal modules.

Results

01

Off-the-shelf VLMs show limited traffic reasoning

Zero-shot VLMs beat random guessing but fall well short of the CLIP+Transformer baseline (72.7% acc.). Chain-of-thought prompting makes things worse — amplifying language priors over visual evidence. Set-of-marks overlays don't help either, likely due to imperfect detection in the VR environment.

02

Fine-tuning closes the gap — and beats the baseline

LoRA fine-tuning on domain-specific QA pairs pushes both models past the specialist baseline. The best configuration for each architecture differs: Qwen benefits from updating cross-modal modules only; VLP does better with both language and cross-modal adaptation.

03

Eye gaze is the most valuable context cue — but only once the model is trained to use it

Zero-shot: ego motion helps most (+3.2% acc.) but gaze signals are noisy without training. After fine-tuning with ego motion + gaze on screen, the model generalizes better to unseen participants, achieving 83.0% accuracy — a 14.5-point improvement over the specialist baseline.
